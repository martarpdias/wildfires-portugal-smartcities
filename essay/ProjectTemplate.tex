\begin{abstract}


Wildfires represent a major environmental and societal challenge in
Portugal, with climate change increasing the frequency and severity
of fire-prone conditions. Effective prevention requires not only the
identification of high-risk areas but also the equitable allocation
of protective infrastructure across territories with different
economic capacities. This study evaluates how effectively Portugal's
Primary Fuel Management Network (RPFGC) covers high wildfire risk
zones and investigates whether municipalities with lower purchasing
power face disproportionate prevention coverage gaps.\\

Using a Data Science and Smart Cities approach, the analysis
integrates wildfire risk data, fuel management infrastructure,
municipal boundaries, and purchasing power indicators through
Python-based geospatial and statistical methods. A composite
wildfire risk model is constructed from five spatial layers and
classified into five risk levels, identifying a total high-risk
area of approximately 4,327~km\textsuperscript{2} across mainland
Portugal.\\

Results show that only 38.6\% of high-risk territory falls within
the 1~km zone of influence of the RPFGC, leaving approximately
2,656~km\textsuperscript{2} unprotected. Two complementary analyses
were conducted to assess whether prevention gaps are concentrated
in economically vulnerable municipalities. The first examines the
\textit{relative} Gap Ratio: the percentage of each
municipality's high-risk area that is unprotected, and finds
no evidence of disproportionate concentration in vulnerable
municipalities (r~=~-0.37, p~$<$~0.001). The second examines
the \textit{absolute} unprotected area: the total
km\textsuperscript{2} of high-risk land without network coverage, and finds a significant positive relationship with
vulnerability (r~=~0.30, p~$<$~0.001), meaning that more
economically vulnerable municipalities have larger absolute
unprotected areas. Together, these results reveal a territorial
inequality that is absolute rather than relative in nature:
while the network covers a comparable share of high-risk
territory across municipalities, the poorest interior
municipalities face a far greater absolute scale of unprotected
land, and lack the economic capacity to compensate for it.\\

This research contributes to the Smart Cities framework by
demonstrating how geospatial intelligence and data-driven analysis
can support the identification of priority municipalities where
wildfire risk, prevention gaps, and economic vulnerability
intersect, enabling more targeted and evidence-based policy
interventions to improve territorial resilience.\\
\\

\end{abstract}

\section{Problem Description and Indicators}





\subsection{Problem Description}



Wildfires constitute one of the most significant environmental and territorial
challenges affecting Portugal. The country is particularly vulnerable to wildfire
events due to a combination of climatic, environmental, and socio-economic factors,
including its Mediterranean climate, recurrent drought conditions, extensive forested
areas, land abandonment processes, and vegetation accumulation. Climate change has
increased wildfire risk across Europe, particularly in Mediterranean regions where
prolonged droughts, heatwaves, and extreme fire weather conditions have become more
frequent \cite{EEAForestFires2021}. According to the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report,
Southern Europe is expected to experience increasing temperatures, more frequent and
intense heatwaves, and more severe drought conditions, all of which contribute to
elevated wildfire risk and longer fire seasons \cite{BednarFriedl2022Europe}. The
structural roots of this vulnerability are also socio-economic in nature: since the
1960s, rural exodus and rapid industrialisation left vast inland areas of northern and
central Portugal depopulated and effectively unmanaged, driving the expansion of
fire-prone shrublands and eucalyptus and pine plantations \cite{EnviroSociety2022}.
Between 1980 and 2023, wildfires in mainland Portugal burned a cumulative total of
approximately 4.99 million hectares: an area equivalent to roughly 54\% of the
national territory \cite{TheSignatureofClimateinAnnualBurnedAreainPortugal}. The 2017 fire season alone set an
all-time record with approximately 540,000 hectares burned, 114 fatalities, over
1,100 injuries, and economic losses exceeding USD~1.2~billion, making it the costliest
natural disaster in the country's insurance history \cite{ThecompoundeventthattriggeredthedestructivefiresofOctober2017inPortugal}. More
recently, the 2024 fire season burned over 135,000 hectares across central and northern
Portugal in a single week \cite{AdvanceReportonForestFiresinEurope}. The OECD has estimated that
wildfires now affect hundreds of thousands of people annually in Portugal, generate
widespread respiratory health impacts, and represent a structural threat to forest
ecosystems, soil integrity, and long-term territorial resilience \cite{OECD2023Portugal}.\\

Prevention, rather than suppression alone, has increasingly been recognised as the
most effective strategy for reducing the frequency and severity of wildfires. In this
context, Portugal's Primary Fuel Management Network (\textit{Rede Primária de Faixas
de Gestão de Combustível}, RPFGC) constitutes the central instrument of national-scale
wildfire prevention. Established under Decree-Law No.~82/2021 and the Integrated Rural
Fire Management System (SGIFR), the RPFGC is designed to create large-scale horizontal
discontinuities across the landscape through the systematic removal or reduction of
vegetation along strategic corridors \cite{ICNF_RPFGC}. Each corridor consists of a
central forest road of approximately 6~metres, flanked by 10-metre strips cleared of
arboreal and shrub cover, and outer 20-metre strips where canopy spacing is maintained
above 4~metres \cite{CmPenamacor2025}. The network is intended to isolate fire
outbreaks, protect roads, critical infrastructure and social facilities, reduce average
burned area by compartmentalising the landscape, and improve firefighter safety
\cite{RecuperarPortugal_C08}. Its national implementation is funded with
120~million Euros through the Portuguese Recovery and Resilience Plan (PRR, Project
C08-i03), targeting the establishment of 21,000~hectares of fuel management strips
across continental Portugal \cite{Transparencia_C08}. Despite its strategic importance,
the implementation of fuel treatments in Portugal has historically suffered from low
execution rates, challenges related to fragmented private land ownership, and
insufficient targeting of the territories at highest structural risk
\cite{FrontiersPortugal2023}. These shortcomings highlight the critical need to
evaluate whether the infrastructure being deployed is spatially aligned with zones of
greatest wildfire risk, and whether its coverage is equitable across municipalities
with differing socio-economic capacities.\\

This study addresses these challenges by adopting a Smart Cities and Data Science
approach to evaluate the spatial effectiveness and equity of Portugal's primary
prevention infrastructure. Smart city frameworks have increasingly been applied to
disaster risk management, leveraging geospatial intelligence, remote sensing, and
data-driven methods to support more transparent, evidence-based, and equitable public
decision-making \cite{IoTSmartCities2024}. In this context, the integration of
wildfire risk modelling with infrastructure coverage analysis and socio-economic
indicators represents a meaningful contribution to territorial resilience. The central
research question guiding this study is: \textit{How effectively does Portugal's
primary fuel management infrastructure cover high wildfire risk zones, are the gaps
in prevention coverage disproportionately concentrated in the most economically
vulnerable municipalities, and what targeted policy interventions could address these
inequalities?} To answer this question, the study pursues three objectives: (1) to
assess the spatial alignment between high wildfire risk areas and the coverage of the
Primary Fuel Management Network, at the national scale; (2) to analyse coverage gaps at the municipal level and
identify the municipalities with the largest proportions of unprotected high-risk
territory; and (3) to examine whether municipalities with lower purchasing power
exhibit systematically higher prevention coverage gaps, thereby providing evidence of
territorial inequalities in wildfire resilience.\\

\subsection{Indicators}



To operationalise the research question, four indicators are defined and applied
throughout the analysis. Together, they enable a quantitative assessment of wildfire
risk exposure, prevention infrastructure coverage, and the socio-economic conditions
of municipalities.\\


\textbf{Wildfire Risk Index.} A composite spatial indicator that quantifies wildfire
risk across mainland Portugal on a scale from 1 (very low) to 5 (very high). It
is constructed by combining five input layers: conjunctural hazard, historical
burned areas, critical fire locations, vegetation index, and aridity index,
through a weighted overlay, where each layer is normalised to a common scale prior
to combination. The primary fuel management network layer is also sourced from the
same institution. For the coverage
analysis, only areas classified as risk class~4 (high) or class~5 (very high)
are considered.\\

\textbf{Coverage Ratio.} The proportion of high-risk territory (classes 4--5)
that falls within the zone of influence of the Primary Fuel Management Network
(RPFGC), defined as a 1~km buffer around the network. A higher value indicates
better alignment between prevention infrastructure and high-risk zones.\\

\textbf{Gap Ratio.} The complement of the Coverage Ratio, quantifying the proportion
of high-risk territory that remains outside the buffer and is therefore considered
unprotected. It is the primary indicator used in the municipal-level equity analysis
to identify territories where prevention coverage is insufficient relative to
wildfire exposure.\\

\textbf{Purchasing Power.} The indicator of economic vulnerability at the municipal
level, specifically the per capita income index (IpC),
measured relative to the national average (base~=~100).
The index is inverted and normalised to a 0--1 scale, where 1 represents the most
economically vulnerable municipality, and used to examine whether prevention coverage
gaps are disproportionately concentrated in economically weaker territories.


\subsubsection*{Ethical and Social Implications}


The indicators selected in this study carry important ethical and social
dimensions that extend beyond their technical definition.\\

The Wildfire Risk Index concentrates the analysis on the territories of
greatest fire danger, ensuring that prevention resources and policy attention
are directed where they are most needed. The weighting scheme assigns the
greatest importance to conjunctural hazard, reflecting the central role of
dynamic climatic and meteorological conditions in determining fire danger,
while historical burned areas and critical locations capture structural and
recurrent risk patterns. The transparency of this construction allows results
to be scrutinised and reproduced, which is itself an ethical requirement of
evidence-based public policy.\\

The Coverage and Gap Ratios translate infrastructure presence into a measurable
assessment of protected versus unprotected territory. This framing is
analytically useful but it is important to acknowledge that proximity to a
fuel management corridor does not guarantee complete protection from wildfire.
The use of a 1~km buffer is a transparent and operationally justified threshold,
supported by sensitivity analysis at 500~m and 2~km, but communities just
outside this boundary may still face significant levels of exposure.\\

The use of purchasing power as a vulnerability indicator reflects a deliberate
choice to foreground economic inequality as a lens for evaluating prevention
equity. This is ethically significant: it positions wildfire resilience not
merely as a technical or environmental problem, but as a social justice issue
in which economically marginalised communities, often rural, elderly, and
with limited institutional capacity, may bear a disproportionate share of
wildfire risk. At the same time, vulnerability is
multi-dimensional, and factors such as population ageing, social isolation,
and access to emergency services are not fully captured by an economic index
alone. Future analyses could benefit from composite vulnerability indicators
that integrate these dimensions more comprehensively.\\

Finally, the municipal scale of analysis is well suited for policy targeting,
as it aligns with the administrative units through which prevention investment
and governance decisions are made in Portugal. While intra-municipal variation
may exist, the municipal level provides the most actionable and institutionally
meaningful unit for identifying priority territories and informing resource
allocation decisions within the Smart Cities framework.

Burned area perimeters record the spatial extent of fire events across
mainland Portugal for each year between 2021 and 2024, totalling 5,998
individual fire perimeters across the four-year period. Conjunctural hazard
maps provide annual raster layers of fire danger conditions derived from
climatic and meteorological variables. Critical fire locations identify areas
of structural susceptibility to wildfire ignition and spread. The vegetation
index and aridity index provide spatial layers of fuel load and drought stress
respectively, both at the polygon level. All five datasets, together with the
Primary Fuel Management Network, were obtained from the Instituto da
Conservação da Natureza e das Florestas (ICNF) \cite{ICNF}.\\

Municipal boundaries were sourced from the "Carta Administrativa Oficial de
Portugal- CAOP" , published by the Direção-Geral do Território (DGT)
\cite{DGT_CAOP}. This layer provides the official administrative boundaries
for all municipalities in mainland Portugal and serves as the spatial unit
for the municipal-level analysis.\\

Purchasing power data were obtained from the INE 2023 Municipal Purchasing
Power Study (\textit{Estudo sobre o Poder de Compra Concelhio}), which
provides the per capita income index (IpC) for each municipality relative
to the national average \cite{INE_PoderCompra}.\\


\subsection{Data Preparation}

\subsubsection*{Data Cleaning}
All datasets were inspected and cleaned before analysis to ensure consistency
and correctness. For the conjunctural hazard rasters, nodata values were
identified and masked separately for each annual layer, since each yearly file
used a different encoding convention: 255 for 2021, no explicit nodata for 2022,
and 15 for 2023 and 2024. Zero-value pixels were also excluded, as they indicate
areas with no recorded hazard rather than genuinely low risk values. For the
social vulnerability data from the INE 2023 Municipal Purchasing Power Study,
values were parsed from a semicolon-delimited CSV file encoded in Latin-1,
decimal commas were converted to floating-point numbers, and rows with missing
values were removed. Municipality names were then normalised through Unicode
accent removal and lowercasing to allow consistent matching between the INE
dataset and the official administrative boundary file, producing a final set
of 278 matched municipalities for the municipal-level analysis.\\
\newpage

\subsubsection*{Spatial Harmonisation}
All datasets were aligned to a common spatial reference system, EPSG:3763
(PT-TM06/ETRS89), the official Portuguese national grid, and resampled to a
uniform 250~m resolution reference grid. The conjunctural hazard rasters were
originally provided at 25~m resolution; to make it computationally feasible
to process multiple large national-scale rasters simultaneously, all rasters
were downsampled by a factor of 10 using \texttt{rasterio}'s out-shape
resampling, dividing the number of pixels by 100 while preserving the spatial
patterns relevant to the analysis. A mainland Portugal boundary mask was
derived from the national administrative boundary shapefile and rasterised
onto the reference grid to restrict all outputs to continental territory,
excluding the Atlantic archipelagos. All vector layers, burned area perimeters,
critical fire locations, vegetation index, aridity index, and the primary fuel
management network, were verified against the reference coordinate system prior
to rasterisation.\\

\subsubsection*{Municipality-Level Integration}
Vector layers were converted to raster format at 250~m resolution using the
hazard layer as the reference grid, via \texttt{rasterio.features.rasterize}.
Categorical attributes were mapped to ordinal numerical values prior to
rasterisation: the vegetation quality index (\textit{Índice de Qualidade da
Vegetação}, IQV) was encoded on a five-point scale from \textit{Baixa}~(Low,
1) to \textit{Muito alta}~(Very High, 5), and the aridity index from
\textit{Húmido}~(Humid, 1) to \textit{Semiárido}~(Semi-arid, 4). Burned
area perimeters for 2021--2024 were merged into a single cumulative binary
layer (burned~=~5, unburned~=~1 on the normalised 1--5 scale). All layers
were then normalised to a common 1--5 scale and combined through a weighted
overlay to produce the composite Wildfire Risk Index, described in detail in
Section~\ref{sec:Wildfire Risk Assessment}. Municipal-level statistics were
subsequently computed by iterating over each municipality polygon, rasterising
its boundary onto the reference grid, and intersecting it with the risk and
coverage layers to extract per-municipality totals. Social vulnerability scores
were joined to the municipal geodataframe using the normalised municipality
names as the common key.\\


\subsection{Wildfire Risk Assessment}


The wildfire risk model is constructed through a weighted overlay of five
spatial input layers, each normalised to a common 1--5 ordinal scale prior
to combination, where 1 represents very low risk and 5 represents very
high risk. Conjunctural hazard is derived as the pixel-wise mean of the
four annual hazard rasters (2021--2024), normalised to a 0--1 scale before
scaling to 1--5. Burned areas are treated as a binary layer: pixels that
burned at least once between 2021 and 2024 receive a value of 5 (maximum
risk), while unburned pixels receive a value of 1 (minimum risk). Critical
fire locations are rasterised as a binary presence layer and subsequently
normalised to the 1--5 scale, where pixels coinciding with a critical
location receive the maximum value. The vegetation index (IQV) and aridity
index are each normalised to 1--5 using min-max normalisation, with zero
values treated as no-data and excluded from the calculation.

The composite risk score is computed as a weighted overlay:

\begin{equation*}
\text{Risk} = 0.35 \times \text{Hazard} + 0.20 \times \text{Critical Locations}
+ 0.20 \times \text{Burned Areas} + 0.15 \times \text{Vegetation}
+ 0.10 \times \text{Aridity}
\end{equation*}

The weights reflect the relative contribution of each factor to wildfire
danger. Conjunctural hazard receives the highest weight (0.35) as it
captures the most dynamic and immediate fire danger conditions driven by
meteorological variables. Burned areas and critical locations each receive
a weight of 0.20, encoding historical fire recurrence and structural
territorial susceptibility respectively. Vegetation index (0.15) reflects
fuel availability, and aridity (0.10) encodes background climatic stress.
Where a layer contains no-data for a given pixel, the remaining layers are
renormalised by their combined weight, ensuring that missing data does not
bias the composite score. The resulting continuous surface is subsequently
discretised into five risk classes using equal interval classification.

\subsection{Prevention Coverage Analysis}

To assess whether a high-risk area is covered by the Primary Fuel
Management Network (RPFGC), a zone of influence is defined around the
network's linear infrastructure using a raster buffer approach. The fuel
network is first rasterised onto the same 250~m reference grid used for
the risk model, assigning a value of 1 to pixels intersecting a network
segment and 0 elsewhere. Buffer zones are then computed via binary
dilation of the rasterised network, expanding the covered area by a
defined number of pixels in all directions. At 250~m resolution, 2 pixels
correspond to 500~m, 4 pixels to 1~km, and 8 pixels to 2~km.

Three buffer distances are tested, 500~m, 1~km, and 2~km, to assess
the sensitivity of coverage results to this methodological choice.
A high-risk pixel (class 4 or 5) is classified as \textit{covered} if it
falls within the buffer zone, and as \textit{uncovered} otherwise.
The 1~km buffer is adopted as the primary threshold for the municipal-level
analysis, as it represents a operationally meaningful distance within which
fuel management infrastructure can reasonably be expected to reduce fire
spread. Figure~\ref{fig:fuel_network} shows the spatial distribution of
the Primary Fuel Management Network across mainland Portugal.

Portugal is not covered by the Primary Fuel Management Network.

\subsection{Economic Vulnerability Analysis}


\subsubsection*{Purchasing Power Distribution}

Economic vulnerability at the municipal level is measured using the
per capita income index (IpC) from the INE 2023 Municipal Purchasing
Power Study (\textit{Estudo sobre o Poder de Compra Concelhio})
\cite{INE_PoderCompra}. The IpC expresses the purchasing capacity of
each municipality's resident population relative to the national
average, which is set at a base value of 100. Municipalities with an
IpC below 100 have below-average purchasing power and are considered
economically vulnerable for the purposes of this study.

The INE dataset is loaded and matched to the municipal boundaries
shapefile by municipality name. To account for differences in
accentuation and capitalisation between the two sources, municipality
names are normalised by removing accents and converting to lowercase
prior to matching.\\

The IpC is inverted and normalised to a 0--1 vulnerability score,
where 1 represents the most economically vulnerable municipality and
0 represents the least vulnerable:

\begin{equation*}
\text{Vulnerability Score} =
1 - \frac{\text{IpC}_i - \min(\text{IpC})}{\max(\text{IpC}) -
\min(\text{IpC})}
\end{equation*}

This transformation ensures that all subsequent analyses use a
consistent and comparable scale across municipalities.

\subsection{Relative and Absolute Coverage Gaps and Economic Vulnerability}



The relationship between prevention coverage gaps and economic
vulnerability is central to the equity dimension of this study.
If municipalities with lower purchasing power systematically exhibit
higher Gap Ratios, this would suggest that the allocation of wildfire
prevention infrastructure is spatially inequitable, leaving the
most economically fragile territories also the least protected. Such
a pattern would have significant implications for public policy, as
it would indicate that communities with the least capacity to
self-protect are also those receiving the least institutional support.
Conversely, if no such relationship exists, it would suggest that
the network is broadly risk-informed rather than socially biased.
To provide a comprehensive assessment, two complementary analyses
are conducted: one examining the \textit{relative} coverage gap: the percentage of each municipality's high-risk area that is
unprotected, and one examining the \textit{absolute} unprotected
area: the total km\textsuperscript{2} of high-risk land without
network coverage. Together, these two perspectives allow for a
more complete understanding of territorial inequalities in wildfire
resilience.\\

computed using the 1~km buffer are joined with the vulnerability
scores derived from the INE 2023 IpC index.
The two indicators: Gap Ratio and Vulnerability Score,  are then

  \item \textbf{High:} Gap Ratio above median or vulnerability above

  \item \textbf{Medium:} Gap Ratio below median and vulnerability

  \item \textbf{Low-Medium:} Gap Ratio above median and vulnerability

\subsection{Results and Discussion}
\label{subsec:results}


\subsubsection*{Coverage Effectiveness}

The integrated wildfire risk model identifies a total high-risk area
(classes 4 and 5) of approximately 4,327~km\textsuperscript{2} across
mainland Portugal, corresponding to roughly 4.7\% of the national
territory. Class~4 (High) accounts for 3,965~km\textsuperscript{2}
and Class~5 (Very High) for 361~km\textsuperscript{2}, while the
majority of the territory falls within lower risk classes
(Figure~\ref{fig:risk_bars}). As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:risk_bars},
classes 4 and 5 together represent only 4.7\% of the national
territory, while classes 1 and 2 account for over 75\%, indicating
that the highest risk levels affect a relatively small but critical
portion of mainland Portugal.

At the national scale, only 38.6\% of high-risk territory falls
within the 1~km zone of influence of the Primary Fuel Management
Network, leaving 61.4\%,  approximately
2,656~km\textsuperscript{2}, unprotected
(Table~\ref{tab:coverage}, Figure~\ref{fig:coverage_stacked}).
Even at the most generous 2~km buffer, 38.5\% of high-risk
territory remains outside the network's zone of influence. These
results indicate that, at the national scale, the current deployment
of the RPFGC covers less than half of the territory most exposed to
wildfire risk, representing a significant gap in prevention
infrastructure. This finding is consistent with the literature
highlighting low execution rates and insufficient targeting in
Portuguese fuel management policy \cite{FrontiersPortugal2023}.

\subsubsection*{Economic Vulnerability and Prevention Gaps}

The economic vulnerability analysis reveals that mainland Portugal
is predominantly characterised by below-average purchasing power,
particularly in the northern and central interior
(Figure~\ref{fig:vulnerability_map}). The most economically
vulnerable municipalities include Tabuaço (IpC~=~63.53,
Vulnerability~=~1.00), Penamacor (IpC~=~63.77,
Vulnerability~=~1.00), Vimioso (IpC~=~63.80,
Vulnerability~=~1.00), and Vinhais (IpC~=~64.26,
Vulnerability~=~0.99), all of which are rural interior
municipalities with significantly below-average economic capacity.

The Pearson correlation between vulnerability score and Gap Ratio
is r~=~-0.37 (p~$<$~0.001), indicating a statistically significant
negative relationship (Figure~\ref{fig:scatter}). In the scatter
plot, each point represents one municipality, with the X axis
showing the vulnerability score and the Y axis showing the Gap
Ratio. The dashed trend line slopes downward from left to right,
meaning that as vulnerability increases, the Gap Ratio tends to
decrease. Contrary to a simple inequality hypothesis, more
economically vulnerable municipalities do not systematically
exhibit larger relative coverage gaps. This is explained by the
spatial concentration of the RPFGC in the northern and central
interior, precisely where both wildfire risk and economic
vulnerability are highest, suggesting that network placement
is broadly risk-informed rather than socially biased.

However, this result must be interpreted alongside the absolute
dimension of coverage gaps. A complementary analysis of the
relationship between vulnerability and absolute uncovered area
yields a positive correlation of r~=~0.30 (p~$<$~0.001)
(Figure~\ref{fig:scatter_absolute}), indicating that more vulnerable
municipalities carry significantly larger absolute unprotected areas.
The two correlations together tell a complete story: the network
broadly follows risk patterns in relative terms, but the absolute
burden of unprotected high-risk territory falls disproportionately
on the most economically fragile municipalities. As shown in
Table~\ref{tab:top10}, the municipalities with the largest absolute
uncovered high-risk areas: Castro Daire
(153~km\textsuperscript{2}, Gap~=~55.4\%, Vulnerability~=~0.96),
Guarda (193~km\textsuperscript{2}, Gap~=~42.9\%,
Vulnerability~=~0.73), Torre de Moncorvo
(95~km\textsuperscript{2}, Gap~=~82.1\%, Vulnerability~=~0.95),
and Montalegre (102~km\textsuperscript{2}, Gap~=~73.7\%,
Vulnerability~=~0.99), are all highly vulnerable interior
municipalities. The network is present in these territories, but
it is not large enough to cover the scale of risk that exists
there. Since these municipalities also lack the economic capacity
to self-protect, the result is a territorial inequality that is
absolute rather than relative in nature.
\subsubsection*{Territorial Inequalities in Wildfire Resilience}

The correlation heatmap (Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}) confirms that
municipalities with larger high-risk areas tend to have slightly
better relative coverage (r~=~0.38), and more vulnerable
municipalities also tend to have marginally better coverage
(r~=~0.37). While this suggests the network broadly follows
risk and vulnerability patterns, it also reveals that a
significant share of high-risk, high-vulnerability territory
remains unprotected in absolute terms.

The spatial distribution of Gap Ratios
(Figure~\ref{fig:municipal_gaps}) reveals a clear geographic
pattern: municipalities with the largest proportions of
unprotected high-risk territory are predominantly located in
the northern and central interior of Portugal, a region
characterised by extensive forested areas, high wildfire
exposure, and low population density. Coastal and southern
municipalities tend to exhibit lower Gap Ratios, reflecting
either lower wildfire risk or better relative coverage by
the network.

Taken together, these results suggest that while the RPFGC
is broadly aligned with territorial risk, its current scale
is insufficient to protect the most exposed and vulnerable
municipalities. A municipality with over 80~km\textsuperscript{2}
of uncovered high-risk area and a vulnerability score above
0.95 faces a fundamentally different challenge than a coastal
municipality with less than 1~km\textsuperscript{2} uncovered,
even if their relative Gap Ratios appear similar. This
distinction between relative and absolute inequality is
central to understanding the territorial resilience challenges
facing Portugal's interior municipalities.

The priority classification identifies 9 municipalities as Very
High priority, 35 as High priority, 92 as Medium, 92 as
Low-Medium, and 44 as Low priority (Figure~\ref{fig:priority_map}).
The 9 Very High priority municipalities combine above-median Gap
Ratios, above-median vulnerability scores, and large high-risk
areas, representing the most urgent cases for targeted prevention
investment. The fact that only 9 municipalities fall into the Very
High category reflects the concentration of the worst-case
combination, high risk, low coverage, and high vulnerability,
in a small number of interior territories, reinforcing the case
for geographically targeted rather than uniform national
intervention.\\



These findings are broadly consistent with the existing
literature on wildfire risk and prevention in Portugal.
Benali et al.\ \cite{FrontiersPortugal2023} highlight the importance
of spatially targeted fuel management interventions at the
local scale, arguing that prevention resources must be
concentrated where structural risk is highest
\cite{FrontiersPortugal2023}. The OECD has similarly
identified the need for risk-informed resource allocation
and improved prevention infrastructure in Portugal's most
exposed territories \cite{OECD2023Portugal}. The negative
correlation between vulnerability and gap ratio found in
this study partially supports the hypothesis that the
network follows risk patterns, but the absolute scale of
unprotected territory in high-vulnerability municipalities
points to a need for substantial expansion of prevention
infrastructure, consistent with the PRR investment targets
\cite{Transparencia_C08}.





\subsection{Policy Interventions and Ethical Implications}

The findings of this study point to a set of concrete and evidence-based
policy interventions to address the identified gaps in wildfire prevention
coverage across mainland Portugal. Each recommendation is grounded in the
analytical results and accompanied by a discussion of its ethical and
social implications.

\subsubsection*{Targeted Expansion of the Primary Fuel Management Network}

At the national scale, the RPFGC covers only 38.6\% of high-risk
territory at the 1~km buffer threshold, leaving approximately
2,656~km\textsuperscript{2} unprotected. The 9 Very High priority
municipalities identified in this study: Fafe, Vimioso,
Idanha-a-Nova, Ponte da Barca, Celorico de Basto, Miranda do Douro,
Póvoa de Lanhoso, Vila Verde, and Penafiel, should be the immediate
focus of prevention investment. These municipalities combine Gap Ratios
above 87\%, vulnerability scores above 0.85, representing the worst-case combination of
exposure, insufficient coverage, and limited economic capacity to
self-protect. The 35 High priority municipalities should constitute
the second tier of intervention.

The PRR project C08-i03 targets the establishment of 21,000~hectares
of fuel management strips \cite{Transparencia_C08}, but the results
of this study suggest that this investment must be spatially targeted
to maximise its impact. Expansion should prioritise municipalities
with Gap Ratios above 90\% such as Fafe, Celorico de Basto, Ponte
da Barca, Vila Verde, and Póvoa de Lanhoso, where the network is
virtually absent from high-risk zones.

From an ethical standpoint, prioritising investment based on a
combination of risk exposure and economic vulnerability is a
redistributive choice that explicitly acknowledges the unequal
capacity of municipalities to respond to wildfire risk. This approach
is consistent with principles of environmental justice and territorial
cohesion, and reflects the responsibility of the state to direct
public resources where they are most needed. Transparent reporting
of coverage gaps at the municipal level, as demonstrated in this
study, is a prerequisite for accountable governance.

\subsubsection*{Risk-Informed Resource Allocation}

The results demonstrate that the current network is broadly
risk-informed, it is concentrated where fire risk and vulnerability
are highest. However, the absolute scale of unprotected territory in
the most exposed municipalities reveals that risk-informed placement
alone is insufficient without adequate investment in network density
and extent. Resource allocation decisions should therefore be guided
by both the Gap Ratio and the absolute uncovered area, ensuring that
municipalities with large high-risk territories receive proportionally
greater investment regardless of their relative coverage performance.

The purchasing power index used in this study provides a practical
proxy for identifying municipalities with limited institutional
capacity, but future resource allocation frameworks should consider
broader vulnerability indicators including population ageing, housing
informality, and access to emergency services, to ensure that no
dimension of social fragility is overlooked.

\subsubsection*{Smart Monitoring and Decision Support Systems}

Within the Smart Cities framework, the geospatial and data-driven
methodology developed in this study can serve as the basis for a
continuous monitoring and decision support system for wildfire
prevention policy in Portugal. By regularly updating the risk model,
coverage analysis, and vulnerability scores with new data, policymakers
can track the evolution of coverage gaps over time, evaluate the
impact of network expansion investments, and identify emerging
priority territories before they become critical. Integration with
real-time sensor data, satellite imagery, and climate projections
would further enhance the predictive capacity of such a system
\cite{IoTSmartCities2024}.

The deployment of such systems raises important ethical considerations
around data governance, transparency, and algorithmic accountability.
Decisions informed by automated models must remain subject to
democratic scrutiny, and the communities most affected by wildfire
risk should have access to the data and analyses that shape prevention
policy in their territories.



\subsection{Conclusion}


This study set out to answer the following research question:
\textit{How effectively does Portugal's primary fuel management
infrastructure cover high wildfire risk zones, are the gaps in
prevention coverage disproportionately concentrated in the most
economically vulnerable municipalities, and what targeted policy
interventions could address these inequalities?}

The analysis shows that the Primary Fuel Management Network covers
only 38.6\% of high wildfire risk territory at the 1~km threshold,
leaving approximately 2,656~km\textsuperscript{2} unprotected. In
relative terms, the evidence does not support the hypothesis that
coverage gaps are disproportionately concentrated in the most
economically vulnerable municipalities (r~=~-0.37, p~$<$~0.001),
indicating that the network broadly follows risk patterns. However,
in absolute terms, more vulnerable municipalities carry significantly
larger unprotected areas (r~=~0.30, p~$<$~0.001), revealing a
territorial inequality that is real but absolute rather than relative
in nature. The absolute burden of unprotected high-risk territory
falls disproportionately on the most economically fragile interior
municipalities, which also lack the capacity to self-protect.

In response to the third component of the research question, the
study identifies 9 Very High priority municipalities: Fafe,
Vimioso, Idanha-a-Nova, Ponte da Barca, Celorico de Basto, Miranda
do Douro, Póvoa de Lanhoso, Vila Verde, and Penafiel, as the
most urgent targets for targeted expansion of the RPFGC. A
risk-informed, equity-conscious, and data-driven approach to
prevention investment, supported by smart monitoring systems,
represents the most effective path towards reducing wildfire risk
and territorial inequality in mainland Portugal.
